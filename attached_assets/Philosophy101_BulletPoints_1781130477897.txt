PHILOSOPHY 101 — FIVE BULLET POINTS PER TOPIC

UNIT 1
===========================================================

1.1 WHAT PHILOSOPHY IS AND HOW IT DIFFERS FROM SCIENCE
- Philosophy delineates the structures of the categories (event, cause, mind, knowledge, truth, justice, etc.) through which we understand the world; this is its sole function.
- We use these categories expertly but understand them poorly; philosophy's job is to make explicit the conditions a thing must satisfy to fall under them — it is meta-knowledge, not first-order knowledge.
- Philosophical puzzles differ from empirical ones: empirical puzzles are settled by gathering more facts, philosophical puzzles by analyzing concepts and structure.
- Philosophy is not merely linguistic analysis; the real objects of analysis are often propositions (sentence-meanings), not sentences.
- Science discovers contingent spatiotemporal facts; philosophy clarifies necessary, structural relations among properties — so its method is a priori conceptual analysis, not empirical observation.

1.2 THE MEANING OF MEANING
- "Meaning" has three distinct senses: evidential (smoke means fire), psychological (what a speaker intends), and linguistic (what a sentence itself says).
- Only the linguistic sense (Meaning #3) directly concerns the nature of language, and it is not reducible to the other two.
- Psychological meaning is parasitic on linguistic meaning: you cannot mean something by a sentence unless you already know what that sentence means.
- Linguistic meaning is not speaker-meaning: sentences never uttered by anyone (e.g., "the cube root of three is Olivier's favorite irrational number between one and four") still have determinate meanings.
- The meaning of a true-or-false sentence is a proposition; propositions are mind-independent abstract structures, isomorphic to but distinct from the sentences that express them.

1.3 LANGUAGE AND THOUGHT
- Knowledge of language sharply enhances intelligence, yet we do NOT think in language — neither in a natural language nor in an innate "language of thought."
- Thought is prior to language: to learn or understand any language you must already be able to make judgments, so thinking is presupposed by linguistic competence.
- Propositions are digital (decompose into discrete units); perceptions are analogue (seamless) — so at least some thought has a non-linguistic, non-digital structure.
- Distinct sentences express one proposition and one sentence carries many meanings — so thoughts cannot be identical with sentence-tokens.
- Language aids thought by supplying a manipulable system of "icons" that integrate otherwise compartmentalized streams of cognition, much as desktop icons let one manage underlying electrical activity.

1.4 DO WE THINK IN WORDS?
- No. We routinely have sophisticated thoughts we cannot put into words, and articulating a thought often requires hard prior thinking — both show thought precedes words.
- The Wittgenstein/McDowell/Blackburn slogan "if you can't say it, you don't know it" is false; knowledge is expressible in drawings, movements, and perceptual discriminations, not only utterances.
- Version 1 (we think in English, Spanish, etc.) fails: speakers of different languages share thoughts (1+1=2), and paraphrase and disambiguation would be impossible if thoughts were sentences.
- Version 2 (we think in an innate code) is viciously circular: understanding any code, even an innate one, requires already grasping meaning-rules — i.e., already thinking.
- It feels as though we think in words because mental imagery (an inner voice) accompanies and helps manage thought, but those feeble images are not the thought itself.

1.5 PERCEPTION AND CONCEPTION
- Perception is purely descriptive: to see an object is to be visually apprised of an existence-claim it uniquely satisfies (there is exactly one thing with such-and-such properties, here, now).
- You never perceive a bare object plus its properties; you perceive organized property- and relation-instances — the idea of perceiving a featureless blank is a non-starter.
- Conception, like perception, is descriptive: to think of a thing is to grasp some existence-claim it uniquely satisfies; there is no non-descriptive thought of an object.
- Semantic rules (e.g., "Fred refers to x") are descriptively threadbare but are always learned through descriptively rich, non-semantic (pre-semantic) perceptual information.
- This is why an utterance can literally mean something spare while conveying (implicating) something far richer — the richness is soaked up from the pre-semantic context of acquisition.

1.6 NAMES, REFERENCE, LITERAL VS. IMPLIED MEANING
- Following Mill, proper names are mere labels: they denote without connoting (the "Dartmouth" keeps naming its river even after the mouth leaves Dart) — names are rigid designators; descriptions are not.
- Definite descriptions are not singular terms; on Russell's Theory of Descriptions, "the phi is psi" means "exactly one thing is phi, and any such thing is psi" — they quantify, they do not refer.
- A name's literal meaning is exhausted by its referent ("Larry is psi" literally means just "x is psi"); everything richer is non-literal implicature.
- Competent speakers tacitly separate literal meaning from implicature: "Larry doesn't have a mustache" is merely false, not self-contradictory — proving "Larry" is synonymous with no description.
- Reference-fixing is not meaning-giving: a description can fix who "Larry" picks out without becoming the name's meaning (which is why Larry might not have had the mustache).

1.7 NECESSITY, POSSIBILITY, AND THE ANALYTIC-SYNTHETIC DISTINCTION
- Necessity and possibility are properties of propositions, not objects — there are no impossible or "merely possible" objects, only propositions that cannot or can be true.
- Necessity does not come in degrees: a proposition is either necessary or it isn't; apparent "degrees" merely reflect how many conditions must hold for the proposition to be true.
- There is NO necessary a posteriori: Kripke's Hesperus/Phosphorus argument commits a massive non-sequitur, confusing the threadbare semantic rule (a name labels x) with the rich pre-semantic information through which it is learned.
- Analytic truth = high-resolution structural truth: a proposition is necessary in virtue of its structure together with the structures of its constituents (its substructure).
- Quine's denial of analyticity is self-defeating (proving it requires the very notion of necessity it rejects) and would entail that nothing confirms anything.

1.8 DEDUCTION, INDUCTION, AND INFERENCE
- An inference forms a new belief on the basis of old ones; deductive inference aims at 100% (entailment), inductive inference at support (confirmation).
- There are two kinds of induction — induction by enumeration and inference to the best explanation — and the latter is fundamental; legitimate enumeration always collapses into it.
- Naked enumerative induction is worthless (the gambler's fallacy shows this); it is rational only insofar as it warrants positing a structure or mechanism that explains the regularity.
- Deduction is ampliative, contrary to the textbook view: the conclusion genuinely amplifies the premises; entailment is not "nothing new."
- Formal logic is largely irrelevant to thought: formal entailment is parasitic on informal/conceptual entailment, and the notion of "mechanical" thought is incoherent.

2.1 KNOWLEDGE AS JUSTIFIED TRUE BELIEF
- Knowledge requires truth, belief, and justification — but Gettier and Russell cases (the Ford promotion, the stopped clock) show that JTB is not sufficient.
- A lucky guess, or a belief reached from false premises that happens to land on truth, is not knowledge even when justified and true.
- The missing fourth condition is that justification and truth be non-accidentally connected.
- The fourth condition: knowledge is hyper-justified true belief — a belief that is justified AND embodies a correct explanation of the very data doing the justifying.
- In the Larry-in-Texas case you know because your belief correctly explains the evidence; in the Martian-reroute variant you don't, because your explanation of that same evidence is wrong.

2.2 SOURCES OF JUSTIFICATION AND TESTIMONY
- There are exactly three ultimate sources of justification: perceptual evidence, introspective evidence, and rational inference.
- Most knowledge combines perception and inference; some purely conceptual knowledge uses inference alone; some self-knowledge is introspective.
- Testimony can yield knowledge, but only if (a) the source is reliable AND (b) the hearer has independent evidence of that reliability (the cult leader vs. the physician).
- Testimony is not a fourth source: it reduces to perception (hearing or reading the words, plus perceiving evidence of reliability) combined with inference.
- Childhood testimony typically begins as mere belief and becomes knowledge later, once we acquire independent corroboration or rediscover the facts ourselves.

2.3 A PRIORI VS. A POSTERIORI KNOWLEDGE
- Analytic (conceptual) knowledge is acquired by analyzing the object of belief — examining the concepts themselves ("circles are figures of uniform curvature").
- Synthetic knowledge has a non-conceptual component; no amount of conceptual analysis settles it ("Rumsfeld was born in the US") — one must consult the world.
- Synthetic/a posteriori knowledge is knowledge of spatiotemporal facts; analytic/a priori knowledge is knowledge of timeless relations among properties.
- There is no necessary a posteriori and no synthetic a priori: the a priori/a posteriori line tracks the analytic/synthetic line.
- A priori knowledge is non-empirical, and non-empirical knowledge is actually a prerequisite for empirical knowledge (see the critique of empiricism).

2.4 KNOWLEDGE BY ACQUAINTANCE, SELF-KNOWLEDGE, INTUITIVE VS. DISCURSIVE KNOWLEDGE
- Awareness of objects largely collapses into knowledge of truths: to be aware of a thing is to know an existence-claim it satisfies — so acquaintance is mostly descriptive.
- The likely exception is direct awareness of one's own conscious states (e.g., intense pain), where appearance and reality merge — genuinely non-descriptive knowledge.
- Some self-knowledge is more direct than external knowledge (a pain just is its appearance), but much self-knowledge is theoretical and indirect.
- Beliefs like "1+1=2" are not constituents of consciousness (there is nothing it is like to have them), so much of the mind is in that sense unconscious — vindicating a core claim of the psychoanalytic tradition.
- Intuitive vs. discursive: Timmy and Jones both know it is a circle, but Jones also knows the grounds of his judgment (self-knowledge) and knows the fact under two descriptions (pictorial and conceptual) — which dissolves Moore's paradox of analysis.

2.5 SKEPTICISM AND THE FOUNDATIONS OF KNOWLEDGE
- Skepticism is doubt, not disbelief; philosophical skepticisms target whole categories (the external world, induction, unconscious states, universals).
- Hume's problem of induction: enumerative induction seems to need the Uniformity Principle, yet the UP can only be known inductively — a vicious circle.
- The operative principle is not the UP but the Minimization of Causal anomalies (MC): prefer the theory that leaves fewer unexplained causal connections.
- MC is analytically true (it is inherent in the very concept of explanation), so it need not be validated inductively — which breaks Hume's circle without begging the question.
- Hume wrongly assumes enumerative induction is primary; in fact it is parasitic on inference to the best explanation, and naked enumeration is just the gambler's fallacy.

2.6 EMPIRICISM VS. RATIONALISM
- Empiricism (all knowledge is sense-based) is indefensible in its strict form; at least some knowledge is non-perceptual.
- We understand the world through universals, which are non-spatiotemporal and causally impotent, hence unperceivable; if empiricism were true we could never justify them, yet they are indispensable — so empiricism fails.
- Not all thoughts are pictorial: conditionals, negations, disjunctions, and dependence-relations cannot be imaged, so thought outruns sense-imagery.
- Images are too specific in some respects and too general in others to be thoughts (any cat-image equals a perfect-impostor image; "all birds" cannot be imaged), and images are not self-referentially true or false, so grasping truth is not sense-based.
- Berkeley unwittingly refutes empiricism (no general idea can be an image), then collapses empiricism into idealism ("to be is to be perceived"); Platonism about properties requires a rationalist epistemology.

3.1 THE MIND-BODY PROBLEM: DUALISM VS. MATERIALISM
- Materialism is probably correct, chiefly because the mental clearly affects the physical, anything that affects the physical is itself physical (Fodor's argument), and such interactions do not violate physical law.
- Epiphenomenalism is false: causation is bidirectional, so if the physical affects the mental, the mental affects the physical.
- The dualist's strongest cards: the mental has properties (intentionality, phenomenology) the physical seems to lack, and some mental items are appearances — and an appearance cannot have hidden parts.
- There is a real explanatory gap (knowing the neuroscience of pain does not tell you what pain is), but, contra Davidson, it does not establish dualism, since identity does not require translatability.
- Behaviorism and functionalism both fail: behaviorism ignores that responses depend on prior mental states; functionalism makes content too brittle and, fatally, strips the mental of causal powers by identifying a state's being-a-belief with its effects.

3.2 FREE WILL, DETERMINISM, AND THE LIBET EXPERIMENT
- Indeterminism does not help freedom — it hurts it: an undetermined choice is rootless, more like a seizure or compulsion than a free act (the man who suddenly decides to become a pilot).
- Determinism is in fact a prerequisite for freedom: if the mechanisms linking choice to action were indeterministic, you would be like someone with Parkinson's — willing but unable.
- Locke/Moore-style compatibilism holds that a free act is one caused by your choice; Frankfurt cases refine but do not refute the core, since a ceteris paribus clause repairs the counterfactual.
- Frankfurt's deeper point: freedom requires acting on second-order desires that align with your first-order ones — being true to who you are, not merely doing what you want.
- The ego-syntonic/ego-dystonic distinction explains it: freedom is identifying with the desires you act on; acting on alien (ego-dystonic) desires forfeits control over your own agency.

3.3 PERSONAL IDENTITY
- Statements of personal identity share the logical structure of object identity: "a = b" asserts either a shared causal sequence or co-instantiated unique properties.
- You are your mind — and even a materialist counts as a mentalist if mental events are identical with patterns of neural activity.
- The strongest physicalist challenge: a person survives a thought-free coma, but nothing that goes out of existence comes back, so if you survive you were not merely your stream of thoughts.
- You are your mind, but your mind is a structure that generates thoughts rather than the thoughts themselves — so it can persist through gaps in consciousness.
- Structure-preserving continuity is the essence of diachronic identity; teleportation and atom-reassembly yield mere duplicates — once gone, always gone.

3.4 CAUSATION
- Only events (and conditions, which themselves consist of events) are causes — objects are not — and nothing non-spatiotemporal, such as a universal, can be a cause.
- A "cause" is really an INUS-condition (Mackie): an insufficient but non-redundant part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition.
- Aristotle's formal and material "causes" are incoherent (dependence-relations hold between events or statements, not things); final causes reduce to efficient causes (a creature's having a goal); only efficient causation is basic.
- Hume's regularity analysis fails three ways: it cannot distinguish real from pseudo-causation (Fido and the earthquake), it makes any adjacent events causal, and it is viciously circular because space and time are themselves causal notions.
- Hume's counterfactual analysis also fails: counterfactuals are disguised causal claims, so the analysis is circular — "E2 wouldn't have occurred without E1" is true because E1 caused E2, not the reverse.

4.1 WHAT ETHICS IS
- Ethics clarifies the structures of normative categories (good, bad, just, commendable) by stating the conditions an act must meet to fall under them.
- Ethics concerns intrinsic, not instrumental, goodness — what is good apart from its consequences.
- Moral attributes are non-privative and non-binary: commendable and condemnable are both positive properties, so a single act can be both, and obligations carry weight or degree.
- "Right" means least bad and "wrong" means least good; an outweighed obligation is not cancelled (stealing to save your children is right yet still leaves a real wrong).
- Key distinctions: legality is not morality; the goodness of an act is not the goodness of the agent (intention fixes praise and blame); and even judging X can be condemnable if done without sufficient information.

4.2 EMOTIVISM, MORAL REALISM, AND FLOURISHING
- Emotivism (value-judgments merely vent emotion) is false: emotions are themselves contentful and doxastic — they embody beliefs about value, so they can be correct or incorrect.
- Moral realism holds: ethical claims carry a factual component (both utilitarian and flourishing analyses make morality a matter of fact), so morality is not mere opinion.
- The one thing with non-derivative moral value is flourishing — the actualization of one's abilities; the value of altruism is derivative of it.
- A self-effacing "moral" life (Smith) can be a worthless life, while a flourishing scoundrel (Brown) can have the better life — the good life means being true to who you are.
- An ethical system that requires you to mutilate your true self is incoherent and likely rooted in aggression; still, flourishing must be distinguished from mere self-indulgence.

4.3 UTILITARIANISM, HEDONISM, EGOISM, AND KANT'S ETHICS
- Psychological hedonism and egoism are false: people pursue activities because they see value in them, and that value is why the activities yield pleasure — not the reverse (Beethoven vs. the heroin user).
- Ethical hedonism and egoism are likewise false and dehumanizing, since they would require the Beethovens of the world to become idlers.
- Kant's two categorical imperatives fail: CI1 (universalizability) and CI2 (treat persons as ends) are not equivalent, both wrongly forbid lying or manipulating even to avert catastrophe, and Kant conflates the good with the praiseworthy.
- Utilitarianism captures a truth (helping is good) but faces fatal counterexamples (transplant, framing); rule-utilitarianism patches these but collapses into either act-utilitarianism or deontology and cannot register intrinsic value.
- Consequentialists and deontologists want the same thing — a world in which rights are not violated — and disagree only about how to achieve it, not about what morality is.

4.4 PHILOSOPHY OF RELIGION AND EXISTENTIALISM
- Philosophy has almost nothing to teach about religion: the famous "paradoxes" (omnipotence, the first cause) are logical puzzles (quantifier scope, the barber) with no bearing on God's existence.
- Religious belief is by its nature not evidence-dependent, so attacking it on scientific or logical grounds begs the question, and liberalizing it ("God is love" or "God is logic") simply abandons it.
- The one genuine contribution is the Euthyphro argument: morality is grounded in the structures of properties, not in God's decrees (even God cannot make rape good, for the same reason He cannot make a square circle).
- Aquinas's first-cause and degree arguments fail on grounds of syntactic ambiguity ("every event has a predecessor" does not entail "some event precedes all events").
- Existentialism is an attitude more than a doctrine; choosing one's values has real merit, but the doctrinalized claim "there are no values one must accept" is false — some values are hardwired into our emotional and biological architecture (compare Chomsky on language).

4.5 PHILOSOPHY OF LAW
- Legality and morality are distinct: acts can be legal yet immoral (antebellum slavery) and illegal yet moral (freeing slaves), though the two often overlap.
- Much talk of "inviolable rights" is best understood in rule-utilitarian terms: a serial killer holds no magic right against planted evidence, but permitting such rule-bending would have disastrous systemic effects.
- National sovereignty is not intrinsically inviolable — it belongs to governments, which are legitimate only insofar as they serve their constituents; "self-determination" abbreviates a consequentialist point.
- Legal systems punish attempts less than completed crimes; since praise and blame track intention, the best rationale is that failure can signal a lack of resolve — an evidential, not a moral-status, difference.
- Law's relation to morality is itself a delicate structural question, handled through the same analysis of normative categories that governs ethics generally.

4.6 CAPSTONE SYNTHESIS
- One thread runs through the system: conception and perception are descriptive, language mirrors thought, and reference is direct — which dissolves the confusions in Frege, Russell on names, and Kripke.
- Platonism about properties grounds the whole edifice: universals are real, non-spatiotemporal, and known a priori, which forces a rationalist epistemology and sinks strict empiricism.
- Necessity, analyticity, and entailment are matters of structure and substructure; deduction is ampliative; and formal logic is parasitic on informal conceptual truth.
- In metaphysics: materialism about mind, determinism as the basis (not the enemy) of freedom, structure-preserving continuity for identity, and causation as real anti-Humean production.
- In value theory: moral realism with flourishing as the sole non-derivative good, and consequentialism and deontology unified — ethics, like the rest of philosophy, is the analysis of the structures of our categories.
