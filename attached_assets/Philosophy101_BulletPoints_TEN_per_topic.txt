1.1 WHAT PHILOSOPHY IS AND HOW IT DIFFERS FROM SCIENCE
- Philosophy delineates the structures of the categories (event, cause, mind, knowledge, truth, justice, etc.) through which we understand the world; this is its sole function.
- We use these categories expertly but understand them poorly; philosophy makes explicit the conditions a thing must satisfy to fall under them — meta-knowledge, not first-order knowledge.
- Philosophical puzzles differ from empirical ones: empirical puzzles are settled by gathering facts, philosophical ones by analyzing concepts and structure.
- Philosophy is not merely linguistic analysis; the real objects of analysis are often propositions (sentence-meanings), not sentences.
- Science discovers contingent spatiotemporal facts; philosophy clarifies necessary structural relations among properties — a priori conceptual analysis, not empirical observation.
- The philosopher seeks the laws governing the laws, standing to the physicist as the physicist stands to the engineer and the engineer to the stranded motorist.
- Philosophy is uniquely "metaknowledge" because it analyzes the preconditions of all knowledge — the analysis of analysis, the logic of logic, the science of science.
- Analytic philosophy follows the rule that when a correct belief seems to entail an absurdity, one clarifies its logical form rather than positing exotic entities.
- Philosophy proceeds by explication, making explicit the previously unrecognized implications already latent in beliefs we hold.
- Unlike the botanist, who prizes the actual over the possible, the philosopher prizes the possible over the actual, using the actual only to illuminate structural necessity.

1.2 THE MEANING OF MEANING
- "Meaning" has three senses: evidential (smoke means fire), psychological (speaker intent), and linguistic (what a sentence says).
- Only the linguistic sense concerns the nature of language, and it is not reducible to the other two.
- Psychological meaning is parasitic on linguistic meaning: you cannot mean something by a sentence unless you already know what it means.
- Linguistic meaning is not speaker-meaning: sentences never uttered by anyone still have determinate meanings.
- The meaning of a true-or-false sentence is a proposition; propositions are mind-independent abstract structures distinct from the sentences expressing them.
- Evidential meaning is a non-causal relation in which one state counts as evidence for another because no reasonable alternative explanation remains.
- A proposition is identical with a set of properties, and its truth consists in the joint instantiatedness of those properties.
- Linguistic meaning is compositional and normative: it is fixed by the meanings of a sentence's parts and their mode of combination, regardless of individual intent.
- The Gricean reduction of meaning to speaker-intention fails because it cannot assign meaning to subsentential expressions like "of" or "or."
- Semantic rules are not psychological reports of what people do mean but rules defining what expressions literally say.

1.3 LANGUAGE AND THOUGHT
- Knowledge of language sharply enhances intelligence, yet we do NOT think in language — neither a natural language nor an innate one.
- Thought is prior to language: linguistic competence presupposes the ability to make judgments.
- Propositions are digital (they decompose into discrete units); perceptions are analogue (seamless).
- Distinct sentences express one proposition and one sentence carries many meanings, so thoughts cannot be identical with sentence-tokens.
- Language aids thought by supplying a manipulable system of "icons" that integrate otherwise compartmentalized streams of cognition.
- Sentences have a unique decomposition into discrete significant parts, whereas images are continuous and lack any finite unit of significance.
- Language enhances intelligence chiefly by letting us label and manipulate abstract principles we can already grasp independently of it.
- Our ability to tell linguistic from non-linguistic behavior shows we grasp a concept in practice before we can state its conditions.
- Language-less creatures can still have discursive thought, since the capacity to judge precedes any capacity to encode judgments in a code.
- Whatever can be grasped through language can be grasped independently of it, because language is a tool for communication and integration, not the medium of thought.

1.4 DO WE THINK IN WORDS?
- No: we routinely have sophisticated thoughts we cannot put into words, and articulating a thought often requires hard prior thinking.
- The slogan "if you can't say it, you don't know it" is false; knowledge shows itself in drawings, movements, and perceptual discriminations.
- Version 1 (we think in English, Spanish, etc.) fails: speakers of different languages share thoughts, and paraphrase and disambiguation would be impossible.
- Version 2 (an innate code) is viciously circular: understanding any code, even an innate one, already requires thinking.
- It feels as though we think in words because mental imagery accompanies thought, but those feeble images are not the thought itself.
- That the same thought is expressible by different sentences (paraphrase) directly refutes the claim that thinking is conducted in a natural language.
- If thought were itself linguistic, the familiar experience of a thought one cannot yet verbalize would be impossible.
- Thinking requires judging whether a code is being applied correctly, a judgment that cannot itself be reduced to more code.
- The "inner voice" is the mind using linguistic icons to stabilize and focus attention during complex reasoning.
- An innate "language of thought" launches a regress, since interpreting the innate code would require a still-prior level of understanding.

1.5 PERCEPTION AND CONCEPTION
- Perception is purely descriptive: to see an object is to grasp an existence-claim it uniquely satisfies.
- You never perceive a bare object plus its properties; you perceive organized property- and relation-instances.
- Conception, like perception, is descriptive: to think of a thing is to grasp some existence-claim it uniquely satisfies.
- Semantic rules are descriptively threadbare but are always learned through descriptively rich, pre-semantic perceptual information.
- An utterance can literally mean something spare while implicating something far richer absorbed from its pre-semantic context.
- Seeing an object involves seeing instances of its morphological, chromatic, and kinematic properties organized into a structure.
- Perception is always of truths or falsehoods (propositions), e.g., that there is a thing of such-and-such a kind over there.
- Every sensory message is consistent with the object's non-existence, so the eyes report property-instantiation, not identity.
- To think about a thing is derivative of propositional thought — really to think that some truth holds concerning it.
- We routinely confuse what the eyes tell us (descriptive information) with what prior knowledge reads into it (identification).

1.6 NAMES, REFERENCE, LITERAL VS. IMPLIED MEANING
- Following Mill, proper names denote without connoting; names are rigid designators, while descriptions are not.
- Definite descriptions are not singular terms; on Russell's Theory of Descriptions, "the phi is psi" quantifies rather than refers.
- A name's literal meaning is exhausted by its referent; everything richer is non-literal implicature.
- Competent speakers separate literal meaning from implicature: "Larry doesn't have a mustache" is merely false, not self-contradictory.
- Reference-fixing is not meaning-giving: a description can fix who "Larry" picks out without becoming the name's meaning.
- Names are rigid because they pick out the same object in every possible world in which that object exists.
- Descriptions like "the author of Hamlet" are non-rigid, since we can conceive of worlds where someone else, or no one, fits them.
- Frege and Russell erred in inferring from the fact that our concepts of people are descriptive that the names for them must be descriptive too.
- "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is informative because the pre-semantic information used to fix the names is smuggled into the communicated message.
- "Socrates died of hemlock" is not a tautology because one can know the semantic rule for "Socrates" without knowing his biography.

1.7 NECESSITY, POSSIBILITY, AND THE ANALYTIC-SYNTHETIC DISTINCTION
- Necessity and possibility are properties of propositions, not of objects — there are no impossible or merely possible objects.
- Necessity does not come in degrees; apparent degrees merely reflect how many conditions must hold for a proposition to be true.
- There is NO necessary a posteriori; Kripke's Hesperus/Phosphorus argument confuses the threadbare semantic rule with the rich pre-semantic information.
- Analytic truth is high-resolution structural truth: a proposition is necessary in virtue of its structure plus its constituents' substructure.
- Quine's denial of analyticity is self-defeating and would entail that nothing confirms anything.
- A statement is analytic when its negation is incoherent or internally inconsistent.
- Formal truth is a special case of analytic truth (true under all interpretations), but not every analytic truth is formal.
- Quine's naturalized epistemology is incoherent, since science cannot scientifically prove that observation is a source of knowledge.
- Informal analytic truths (e.g., "triangles have three sides") turn on the meanings of their terms rather than on logical form alone.
- The analytic-synthetic distinction must be kept, since without it we cannot mark the difference between clarifying a concept and discovering a fact.

1.8 DEDUCTION, INDUCTION, AND INFERENCE
- An inference forms a new belief on the basis of old ones; deductive inference aims at entailment, inductive inference at confirmation.
- There are two kinds of induction — enumeration and inference to the best explanation — and the latter is fundamental.
- Naked enumerative induction is worthless (the gambler's fallacy) unless it warrants positing a structure that explains the regularity.
- Deduction is ampliative, contrary to the textbook view: the conclusion genuinely amplifies the premises.
- Formal logic is largely irrelevant to thought; formal entailment is parasitic on informal, conceptual entailment.
- Deduction expands explicit knowledge precisely by revealing implications we had not yet recognized.
- Inference to the best explanation works by minimizing causal anomalies to settle on the most probable explanation of the data.
- Applying a logical rule or algorithm still requires an underlying act of judgment that only a thinking being can perform.
- An inference is deductive only when the truth of the premises necessitates the truth of the conclusion by structural entailment.
- The gambler's fallacy shows that mere counting of instances yields no predictive power without belief in an underlying structure.

2.1 KNOWLEDGE AS JUSTIFIED TRUE BELIEF
- Knowledge requires truth, belief, and justification — but Gettier and Russell cases (the Ford promotion, the stopped clock) show JTB is not sufficient.
- A lucky guess, or a belief reached from false premises that happens to land on truth, is not knowledge.
- The missing fourth condition is that justification and truth be non-accidentally connected.
- Knowledge is hyper-justified true belief: justified AND embodying a correct explanation of the very data doing the justifying.
- In the Larry-in-Texas case you know because your belief correctly explains the evidence; in the Martian-reroute variant you don't, because your explanation is wrong.
- A belief produced by intellectual ability rather than luck or prejudice is thereby justified, so justification is built into genuine discovery.
- Procedural knowledge (knowing how) is bona fide knowledge that has been integrated deeply enough to be mobilized automatically.
- The gap between knowledge and philosophy is the gap between knowing through the categories and having meta-knowledge about them.
- One cannot accept a proposition without grasping it; merely having it described to you is not belief.
- Intuitive breakthroughs count as knowledge because a correct belief reached by intuition rather than luck is justified by the logic embodied in the discovery.

2.2 SOURCES OF JUSTIFICATION AND TESTIMONY
- There are exactly three ultimate sources of justification: perceptual, introspective, and rational inference.
- Most knowledge combines perception and inference; some purely conceptual knowledge uses inference alone; some self-knowledge is introspective.
- Testimony yields knowledge only if the source is reliable AND the hearer has independent evidence of that reliability (the cult leader vs. the physician).
- Testimony is not a fourth source: it reduces to perception (hearing or reading the words, plus evidence of reliability) combined with inference.
- Childhood testimony typically begins as mere belief and becomes knowledge later, once corroborated or rediscovered independently.
- Intersubjective corroboration is a primary way the merely suggested content of testimony is converted into justified belief.
- We trust intuitions because later data vindicate them, but they remain "intuitions" only while their justifications stay unarticulated.
- An "unjustified belief" may mean either a belief with no basis or one whose existing justification has simply not been stated.
- Justification is often encoded in the very thoughts that produced the belief, so justification is frequently inseparable from discovery.
- For testimony to transmit knowledge, the hearer must have good reason to authenticate the speaker, or the speaker's justification never becomes the hearer's.

2.3 A PRIORI VS. A POSTERIORI KNOWLEDGE
- Analytic (conceptual) knowledge is acquired by analyzing the object of belief (circles are figures of uniform curvature).
- Synthetic knowledge has a non-conceptual component; no conceptual analysis settles it (whether Rumsfeld was born in the US).
- Synthetic/a posteriori knowledge is of spatiotemporal facts; analytic/a priori knowledge is of timeless relations among properties.
- There is no necessary a posteriori and no synthetic a priori: the a priori/a posteriori line tracks the analytic/synthetic line.
- A priori knowledge is non-empirical, and non-empirical knowledge is itself a prerequisite for empirical knowledge.
- A priori knowledge is available merely in virtue of how the mind is structured, like software bundled with a new computer.
- Kripke's "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is not necessary a posteriori, since the literal identity is the a priori, analytic truth that x = x.
- The illusion of necessary a posteriori truths arises from conflating a sentence's literal meaning with its contingent connotations.
- Rigid designators pick out the same object in all possible worlds because their semantic rules do not depend on the object's properties.
- Since names refer directly, an identity between two names is either an analytic triviality or it communicates a contingent description.

2.4 KNOWLEDGE BY ACQUAINTANCE, SELF-KNOWLEDGE, INTUITIVE VS. DISCURSIVE
- Awareness of objects largely collapses into knowledge of truths, so acquaintance is mostly descriptive and propositional.
- The likely exception is direct awareness of one's own conscious states (intense pain), where appearance and reality merge.
- Some self-knowledge is more direct than external knowledge, but much self-knowledge is theoretical and indirect.
- Beliefs like "1+1=2" are not constituents of consciousness, so much of the mind is unconscious — vindicating a core claim of psychoanalysis.
- Intuitive vs. discursive: Timmy and Jones both know it is a circle, but Jones also knows the grounds and the fact under two descriptions, dissolving Moore's paradox of analysis.
- Discursive thought is remedial; thought is most efficient precisely insofar as it remains intuitive.
- To "know" a person is usually a derivative way of saying one knows many specific truths about that person.
- Even acquaintance-talk reduces to descriptive recognition of property-instances rather than contact with bare objects.
- Because every sensory message is consistent with non-existence, the object of thought is always a propositional existence-claim, not the thing itself.
- An intuition is a belief whose justification is currently unknown to the holder, even though that justification may exist and be valid.

2.5 SKEPTICISM AND THE FOUNDATIONS OF KNOWLEDGE
- Skepticism is doubt, not disbelief; philosophical skepticisms target whole categories (the external world, induction, unconscious states, universals).
- Hume's problem of induction: enumerative induction seems to need the Uniformity Principle, yet the UP can only be known inductively — a vicious circle.
- The operative principle is not the UP but the Minimization of Causal anomalies (MC): prefer the theory leaving fewer unexplained connections.
- MC is analytically true (inherent in the very concept of explanation), so it needs no inductive validation, which breaks Hume's circle without begging the question.
- Hume wrongly assumes enumerative induction is primary; in fact it is parasitic on inference to the best explanation.
- Skepticism about the external world is the doubt that perception represents reality rather than a vast, consistent hallucination.
- Minimizing causal anomalies is not a rule of thumb but a requirement of the logic of explanation: prefer fewer unexplained explainers.
- Direct knowledge is known without inference (like pain); indirect knowledge must be figured out by deductive or non-deductive reasoning.
- Incoherent demands (like a "square circle") mark pseudo-tasks that fail to express any possible state of affairs, not limits on power.
- Descartes' demand for rigorous establishment pushes toward axiomatizing knowledge to show our representations are not mere dreams.

2.6 EMPIRICISM VS. RATIONALISM
- Strict empiricism (all knowledge is sense-based) is indefensible; at least some knowledge is non-perceptual.
- We understand the world through universals, which are non-spatiotemporal, causally impotent, and unperceivable, yet indispensable — so empiricism fails.
- Not all thoughts are pictorial: conditionals, negations, disjunctions, and dependence-relations cannot be imaged.
- Images are too specific in some respects and too general in others, and are not self-referentially true or false, so grasping truth is not sense-based.
- Berkeley unwittingly refutes empiricism (no general idea is an image), then collapses it into idealism; Platonism about properties requires a rationalist epistemology.
- A Platonist ontology — spatiotemporal things as instances of non-spatiotemporal properties — forces a rationalist epistemology.
- Properties must exist because common predication ("Sally and Fred are both intelligent") commits us to a shared entity not identical with its instances.
- Anti-Platonist reductions fail because co-extensive properties (creature with a heart vs. with a kidney) are distinct even if their instances coincide.
- Higher-order properties (such as "being uninstantiated") show the realm of properties exists independently of any spatiotemporal members.
- Conceptualism is false, since properties such as the resemblance among rocks existed before there were any minds to conceive them.

3.1 THE MIND-BODY PROBLEM: DUALISM VS. MATERIALISM
- Materialism is probably correct: the mental affects the physical, anything that affects the physical is itself physical (Fodor's argument), and such interactions do not violate physical law.
- Epiphenomenalism is false: causation is bidirectional, so if the physical affects the mental, the mental affects the physical.
- The dualist's strongest cards: the mental has properties (intentionality, phenomenology) the physical seems to lack, and an appearance cannot have hidden parts.
- There is a real explanatory gap, but, contra Davidson, it does not establish dualism, since identity does not require translatability.
- Behaviorism and functionalism both fail: behaviorism ignores prior mental states, and functionalism strips the mental of causal powers.
- On the favored view, mental states are identical with neural events or structures, making the mental a subset of the physical world.
- Intentionality is the property of being "about" something, which the dualist insists no purely physical arrangement could possess.
- The explanatory gap is met by identifying the mind with the enduring structure that mediates mentation, not with the passing stream of thoughts.
- A materialist still counts as a mentalist when mental events are identified with neural patterns, grounding the self in stable personality architecture.
- Behaviorism founders because beliefs and intentions are not mere dispositions to behave; responses depend on prior mental states.

3.2 FREE WILL, DETERMINISM, AND THE LIBET EXPERIMENT
- Indeterminism does not help freedom — it hurts it: an undetermined choice is rootless, more like a seizure than a free act.
- Determinism is in fact a prerequisite for freedom: indeterministic mechanisms would leave you willing but unable, like someone with Parkinson's.
- Locke/Moore-style compatibilism holds that a free act is one caused by your choice; Frankfurt cases refine but do not refute the core.
- Frankfurt's deeper point: freedom requires acting on second-order desires that align with your first-order ones.
- The ego-syntonic/ego-dystonic distinction explains it: freedom is identifying with the desires you act on.
- Determinism is the thesis that the state of the world at a time fixes the entire future, so each choice flows from the distant past.
- Freedom is not the absence of causes but the presence of the right causes — your own choices and desires.
- Acting on a desire you reject (ego-dystonic) is unfree even when the act originates within you.
- A free agent acts on first-order desires that he also wants to have (the relevant second-order desires).
- Unpredictability of behavior is no evidence of indeterminism, since even deterministic systems can be logically unpredictable.

3.3 PERSONAL IDENTITY
- Statements of personal identity share the logical structure of object identity: a shared causal sequence or co-instantiated unique properties.
- You are your mind — and even a materialist counts as a mentalist if mental events are identical with patterns of neural activity.
- The strongest physicalist challenge: a person survives a thought-free coma, yet nothing that goes out of existence comes back.
- You are your mind, but your mind is a structure that generates thoughts rather than the thoughts themselves, so it persists through gaps in consciousness.
- Structure-preserving continuity is the essence of diachronic identity; teleportation and atom-reassembly yield mere duplicates.
- Numerical identity (being one and the same thing) is distinct from qualitative identity (exact similarity); only the former is required for persistence.
- Diachronic identity consists in causal succession with robust explanatory and predictive connectedness, and so it admits of degrees.
- The "ice-man" type case shows a sentient creature survives an interruption of mental activity provided its structure stays intact.
- Personal identity is not a four-dimensional "space-time worm," a view that denies genuine change and mishandles fission cases.
- To be identical with your past self is to be its successor in a continuous causal sequence, not to share all properties at once.

3.4 CAUSATION
- Only events (and conditions, which themselves consist of events) are causes — objects are not — and nothing non-spatiotemporal can be a cause.
- A "cause" is really an INUS-condition (Mackie): an insufficient but non-redundant part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition.
- Aristotle's formal and material "causes" are incoherent; final causes reduce to efficient causes, so only efficient causation is basic.
- Hume's regularity analysis fails three ways: it cannot distinguish real from pseudo-causation, it makes adjacent events causal, and it is circular because space and time are causal notions.
- Hume's counterfactual analysis also fails: counterfactuals are disguised causal claims, so the analysis is circular.
- Persistence and spatial occupancy are themselves causally loaded, so the idea of a causally sterile world of pure sequence is incoherent.
- A causal sequence is a continuous series in which each event causes its successor, and such sequences fix the boundaries of objects.
- Evidential meaning is often underwritten by causation but is not identical with it, since effects can be evidence of their causes.
- Causation cannot reduce to mere sequence, because spatial and temporal separation require a continuous chain linking cause to effect.
- The unobservability of causal "glue" is no more an objection to causation than the unobservability of electrons is an objection to them.

4.1 WHAT ETHICS IS
- Ethics clarifies the structures of normative categories (good, bad, just, commendable) by stating the conditions an act must meet to fall under them.
- Ethics concerns intrinsic, not instrumental, goodness — what is good apart from its consequences.
- Moral attributes are non-privative and non-binary: commendable and condemnable are both positive properties, so one act can be both.
- "Right" means least bad and "wrong" means least good; an outweighed obligation is not cancelled (stealing to save your children is right yet still leaves a real wrong).
- Key distinctions: legality is not morality; the goodness of an act is not the goodness of the agent (intention fixes praise and blame); and judging without sufficient information can be condemnable.
- Ethics adds no new facts to the sciences; it analyzes the structures of concepts like "good" and "just."
- Descriptive statements say how things are, whereas normative statements register satisfaction or violation of a standard.
- A moral law can hold categorically and still be overridden by a weightier obligation in a particular case.
- An act is intrinsically good when its goodness does not depend on consequences or the agent's practical interests.
- The goodness of an agent tracks intention, while the goodness of an act tracks conformity to normative structures.

4.2 EMOTIVISM, MORAL REALISM, AND FLOURISHING
- Emotivism is false: emotions are themselves contentful and doxastic — they embody beliefs about value, so they can be correct or incorrect.
- Moral realism holds: ethical claims carry a factual component, so morality is not mere opinion.
- The one thing with non-derivative moral value is flourishing — the actualization of one's abilities; the value of altruism is derivative of it.
- A self-effacing "moral" life (Smith) can be a worthless life, while a flourishing scoundrel (Brown) can have the better life.
- An ethical system that requires you to mutilate your true self is incoherent; still, flourishing must be distinguished from mere self-indulgence.
- Because emotions are essentially value-beliefs, one can sincerely judge "x is good" coolly and without any feeling.
- Moral truths supervene on our psychological-biological structure, so the moral facts are fixed once the biological and psychological facts are.
- Altruism has value only insofar as it facilitates the flourishing of others, not intrinsically.
- A world of milquetoast do-gooders who never act on self-interest would be immoral, since it would block genius and flourishing.
- Moral truth is not made by opinion, culture, or legislation but is grounded in the fixed capacities and vulnerabilities of human nature.

4.3 UTILITARIANISM, HEDONISM, EGOISM, AND KANT'S ETHICS
- Psychological hedonism and egoism are false: people pursue activities because they see value in them, and that value is why the activities yield pleasure (Beethoven vs. the heroin user).
- Ethical hedonism and egoism are likewise false and dehumanizing, since they would require the Beethovens of the world to become idlers.
- Kant's two categorical imperatives fail: they are not equivalent, both wrongly forbid lying or manipulating even to avert catastrophe, and Kant conflates the good with the praiseworthy.
- Utilitarianism captures a truth (helping is good) but faces fatal counterexamples (transplant, framing); rule-utilitarianism collapses into act-utilitarianism or deontology.
- Consequentialists and deontologists want the same thing — a world in which rights are not violated — and disagree only about how to achieve it.
- The doctrine of double effect distinguishes intended results from merely foreseen ones, as when a doctor causes pain in order to save a life.
- Kant wrongly models morality on legality, assuming a moral law holds only if it dictates the outcome of an action.
- Rationality and autonomy are at bottom the same: autonomy is deciding how to live, rationality deciding how to think.
- Kant's argument for the rationality of morality fails because it equivocates on what it is to "universalize" a principle.
- An autonomy-based principle is a better approximation of moral truth, licensing intervention only to prevent greater violations of autonomy.

4.4 PHILOSOPHY OF RELIGION AND EXISTENTIALISM
- Philosophy has almost nothing to teach about religion: the famous "paradoxes" (omnipotence, the first cause) are logical puzzles with no bearing on God's existence.
- Religious belief is by its nature not evidence-dependent, so attacking it on scientific or logical grounds begs the question, and liberalizing it abandons it.
- The one genuine contribution is the Euthyphro argument: morality is grounded in the structures of properties, not in God's decrees (even God cannot make rape good, just as He cannot make a square circle).
- Aquinas's first-cause and degree arguments fail on grounds of syntactic ambiguity.
- Existentialism is an attitude more than a doctrine; choosing one's values has real merit, but the claim "there are no values one must accept" is false (some are hardwired).
- Paradoxes of omnipotence, like the stone too heavy to lift, are quantifier puzzles that apply to any agent, not specially to a deity.
- Aquinas's first-cause argument conflates "every event has a predecessor" with "some event precedes all events."
- Existentialism is right that choosing values rather than merely inheriting them yields a freer, more psychologically viable life.
- The doctrine "there are no values one must accept" is refuted by values hardwired into our emotional and biological architecture (compare Chomsky on language).
- Philosophy of religion has grown overly technical, often losing sight of religion in favor of logical exercises.

4.5 PHILOSOPHY OF LAW
- Legality and morality are distinct: acts can be legal yet immoral (antebellum slavery) and illegal yet moral (freeing slaves), though the two often overlap.
- Much talk of "inviolable rights" is best understood in rule-utilitarian terms: rule-bending that seems locally harmless would have disastrous systemic effects.
- National sovereignty is not intrinsically inviolable; it belongs to governments, which are legitimate only insofar as they serve their constituents.
- Legal systems punish attempts less than completed crimes; the best rationale is evidential — failure can signal a lack of resolve — not a difference in moral status.
- Law's relation to morality is itself a delicate structural question, handled through the same analysis of normative categories that governs ethics.
- A legal system supplies the structure needed to protect basic rights and coordinate society.
- Anything failing certain minimal moral requirements is, by the very concept of law, not a legitimate legal system.
- The moralistic thesis holds that law is not merely power or coercion but is bound up with moral protection.
- National sovereignty is a derivative right, holding only to the extent a state actually protects its citizens' interests.
- Legal positivism wrongly severs law from morality, missing that legal obligations are a species of moral obligation.

4.6 CAPSTONE SYNTHESIS
- One thread runs through the system: conception and perception are descriptive, language mirrors thought, and reference is direct — which dissolves the confusions in Frege, Russell on names, and Kripke.
- Platonism about properties grounds the whole edifice: universals are real, non-spatiotemporal, and known a priori, which forces a rationalist epistemology and sinks strict empiricism.
- Necessity, analyticity, and entailment are matters of structure and substructure; deduction is ampliative; and formal logic is parasitic on informal conceptual truth.
- In metaphysics: materialism about mind, determinism as the basis (not the enemy) of freedom, structure-preserving continuity for identity, and causation as real anti-Humean production.
- In value theory: moral realism with flourishing as the sole non-derivative good, and consequentialism and deontology unified.
- Philosophy throughout is the delineation of the structures of categories like person, event, knowledge, and value-judgment.
- Propositions are identical with sets of properties, and truth is the joint instantiatedness of those properties.
- Thought is independent of language, and linguistic meaning is fixed by a non-psychological, external dimension.
- Knowledge is not bare justified true belief but true belief whose justification correctly explains the relevant data.
- The whole system rests on the recognition that logical form routinely diverges from surface grammatical form, demanding rigorous analysis.
